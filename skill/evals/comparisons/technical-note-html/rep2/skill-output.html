Constraint scoring note

2026-10-10 · 6 min read · status: draft · source: technical-note.md

A candidate is one proposed solution, such as one setting of a design's parameters, and a constraint is a condition that solution has to satisfy. In this note we do not know for certain whether a candidate satisfies its constraints. For each constraint we have only a probability that it does, and the question is how to turn those separate probabilities into one number per candidate.

The note describes that number, the feasibility score: how it is calculated, how the batch implementation computes it for 32 candidates at once, and what the score does and does not promise. The batch size is 32, and the reason for that choice is not recorded. The evaluation cases accompany this note. See Feasibility for the probability calculation.

Candidates, constraints, and satisfaction probabilities

Each constraint is written as a function gjg_j of the candidate xx, and constraint jj counts as satisfied when gj(x)≤0g_j(x) \leq 0. Writing every constraint this way turns each one into the same sign test, whatever it originally measured. As a hypothetical example, if a part must weigh at most 2 kg, then g_j(x) = weight(x) − 2 is at or below zero exactly when the part is light enough.

There are mm constraints in total, indexed jj from 1 to mm. In the batch implementation below, mm is 3.

The note works with probabilities rather than yes-or-no answers. For each constraint, P(gj(x)≤0∣x)P(g_j(x)\leq 0\mid x) is the probability that constraint jj is satisfied, given the candidate xx. Where these per-constraint probabilities come from is outside the note; they are its inputs. A candidate is feasible when it satisfies all mm constraints at once, and the feasibility score is the probability of that.

Feasibility

The feasibility score multiplies the mm per-constraint probabilities:

P(feasible∣x)=∏j=1mP(gj(x)≤0∣x)P(\mathrm{feasible}\mid x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}P(g_j(x)\leq 0\mid x)

The left side is the probability that candidate xx satisfies every constraint. The right side is one factor per constraint, each the probability of satisfying that constraint alone.

Multiplying is correct only under an assumption: the mm constraint events are independent, conditional on the candidate xx. In words, once the candidate is fixed, learning that constraint 1 is satisfied tells you nothing new about whether constraint 2 is. When the events are dependent, this calculation can be invalid; the next subsection shows how far off it can be.

As a worked instance with hypothetical values, take three constraints with satisfaction probabilities 0.9, 0.8, and 0.5:

P(feasible∣x)=0.9×0.8×0.5=0.36P(\mathrm{feasible}\mid x)=0.9\times 0.8\times 0.5=0.36

Two properties of the product are worth keeping in mind. Every factor is at most 1, so each added constraint can only lower the score or leave it unchanged. And the score can never exceed its smallest factor, so the least likely constraint (0.5 here) caps what any candidate can score.

When the constraints are not independent

Take two constraints that each hold with probability 0.8 (hypothetical). Under independence, the probability that both hold is 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64. Now suppose both constraints depend on the same underlying quantity, say a part's weight, so that whenever one holds the other does too; then the probability that both hold is 0.8, not 0.64. Or suppose the two pull in opposite directions and rarely hold together; then the probability of both can be as low as 0.6.

Those two extremes are the general limits. For two events with probabilities p1p_1 and p2p_2, the probability that both occur must satisfy

max⁡(0, p1+p2−1) ≤ P(both satisfied∣x) ≤ min⁡(p1,p2)\max(0,\;p_1+p_2-1)\;\le\;P(\text{both satisfied}\mid x)\;\le\;\min(p_1,p_2)

The upper bound holds because "both satisfied" is a sub-case of "constraint 1 satisfied" and of "constraint 2 satisfied". The lower bound follows from P(A and B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A or B) together with P(A or B) ≤ 1.

The independence product p1p2p_1 p_2 always lies inside this range, but the true value can sit anywhere in it, so independence can overestimate or underestimate feasibility.

With mm constraints the same reasoning gives a lower bound of max⁡(0, p1+⋯+pm−(m−1))\max(0,\;p_1+\cdots+p_m-(m-1)) and an upper bound of min⁡jpj\min_j p_j, and the product still sits somewhere inside. The note does not say whether the constraints it scores are independent, so treat the score as what it is: the value the product gives under that assumption.

Batch operation

The implementation scores 32 candidates at once. Its input, probabilities, is an array of shape (32, 3): one row per candidate and one column per constraint, with each cell holding that candidate's satisfaction probability for that constraint. Its output is a score vector of shape (32,), one feasibility score per candidate. The batch size is 32, and the reason for that choice is not recorded.

The function is two lines:

def feasibility_score(probabilities):
    return probabilities.prod(axis=1)

prod(axis=1) multiplies along axis 1, the constraints, and keeps axis 0, the candidates. That is why the result has shape (32,) rather than a single number: the array drops one axis, not both.

The function applies the product without establishing whether the independence assumption is justified for its inputs. Nothing in the input could support that check, because the array holds only each constraint's own probability, and those marginal probabilities do not determine how the constraint events depend on each other. Whether the assumption is reasonable has to be settled outside this function.

What the score does and does not promise

The result is a score under the stated independence assumption. It is not a guarantee that the selected candidate will satisfy the constraints, for two separate reasons.

First, even when the assumption holds, the score is a probability. A candidate scoring 0.36 fails at least one constraint with probability 0.64, and the highest-scoring candidate in a batch can still turn out infeasible. Second, when the assumption does not hold, the 0.36 itself can be off, in either direction, by as much as the bounds in the subsection above allow.
