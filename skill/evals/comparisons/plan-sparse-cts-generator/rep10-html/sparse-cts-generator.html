Sparse CTS Generator

Source: docs/plan/sparse_cts_plan.md, sections 6.1–6.3 (lines 1098–1192), as of 2026-10-08. Rewritten for a colleague new to the study.

These three sections of the plan ask how many coordinates one candidate should move at once, and what that choice does and does not control. The setting is candidate generation inside a trust-region Bayesian optimizer: at each step the optimizer proposes a pool of candidate points near the best point found so far, and a selection step picks one of them to evaluate. The plan compares two existing ways of proposing a candidate, CTS and RAASP, and introduces a sparse construction between them with a single integer \(k\).

The plan labels each claim by its status, and the labels are kept here where the plan put them. Fact marks a result that follows from the stated model. Hypothesis marks a conjecture the study will test. Literature context to verify marks a description of published work that still has to be checked against the paper. References such as §2, §4, Step 0, and Step 3 point to other parts of the full plan, which this excerpt does not include. Numerical examples added in this rewrite are hypothetical and are marked as such.

6.1 The generator, and what \(k\) means

CTS builds each candidate from a direction and a distance chosen separately. Starting from the incumbent \(c\), the best point found so far, it picks a unit direction \(v\) and an independent distance \(r\), and moves

$$ x = c + r v, \qquad r \sim U(0, R), \qquad \lVert v \rVert_2 = 1, $$

with \(c\) the incumbent, \(v\) a dense direction and \(r\) an independent distance. The direction is dense in the sense that, in general, every one of its \(d\) coordinates is nonzero, so the move touches all coordinates at once. The distance is uniform between 0 and a cap \(R\), and it is drawn without reference to the direction.

Two details of CTS deal with the walls of the search box. CTS draws \(v\) from a truncated multivariate normal, so that a dense random direction does not point into a nearby wall. It also caps the permissible radius by both the wall distance and a trust-region-like \(R_{\max}\).

Literature context to verify the exact construction, Rashidi, Johnstonbaugh & Gao 2024.

RAASP, the perturbation scheme in the original TuRBO implementation, makes the opposite choice and decides coordinate by coordinate. Each coordinate is perturbed with probability \(\min(1, 20/d)\), and the perturbed coordinates are filled from Sobol points inside the rectangular trust region. For \(d \gg 20\) about 20 coordinates move. Hypothetical instance: at \(d = 500\) each coordinate moves with probability \(0.04\), so a typical candidate moves about 20 of its 500 coordinates.

The sparse construction sits between them. Choose a support \(S \subseteq \{1, \ldots, d\}\) with \(\lvert S \rvert = k\): the set of coordinates allowed to move. Set \(v_i = 0\) for \(i \notin S\), then draw the direction on \(S\) and the radius as CTS does. Hypothetical instance: with \(d = 500\) and \(k = 3\), the candidate moves a distance \(r\) along a unit direction that lives in three chosen coordinates, and the other 497 coordinates keep their incumbent values.

The value of \(k\) then places the move between the two existing samplers:

  • \(k = 1\) is a coordinate-like move;
  • \(1 < k < d\) is a sparse move;
  • \(k = d\) is ordinary CTS in the interior (§2), away from the walls where the truncation acts.

The point to hold on to, a Fact: \(k\) is an angular support dimension, not a distance. It says how many coordinates the direction may use. How far the candidate travels is set by \(r\), separately.

6.2 Why RAASP's “20” cannot be inherited

In a coordinate-wise sampler, moving more coordinates also moves the candidate farther, so a count such as 20 is a distance setting as well. To see this, take a simplified model in which each of the \(k\) perturbed coordinates is displaced independently by \(\delta_i \sim U(-h, h)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\delta_i^2] = h^2 / 3\) and

$$ \mathbb{E}\lVert \delta \rVert_2^2 = \frac{k h^2}{3}, $$

so the typical step grows like \(\sqrt{k}\). Hypothetical instance with \(h = 1\): moving 4 coordinates gives a typical step of about 1.15, moving 16 gives about 2.31. Four times the coordinates, twice the distance. Increasing \(k\) therefore changes how many coordinates move and how far the candidate moves at once.

That is a Fact about this simplified model, not a claim about every RAASP implementation. But it is one reason sparsity helps RAASP stay local, and it is why the TuRBO value 20 belongs to a sampler in which support size also sets distance. In that sampler the number sets two things at once, so it cannot simply be carried over to a sampler in which it sets only one.

The unit-direction construction separates the two. With \(\delta = r v\) and \(\lVert v \rVert = 1\),

$$ \lVert \delta \rVert_2 = r $$

exactly, so with \(r \sim U(0, R)\) the mean squared step is \(R^2 / 3\) for every \(k\) (Fact, in the interior). The two controls are then separate: \(k\) is how many coordinates cooperate, \(r\) is how far the move goes.

Hypothesis (the thread's first): some of the effect commonly attributed to sparse perturbations is due to the support size itself rather than to the shorter steps that coordinate-wise sparsity produces. The test is to hold the radial law fixed, that is, the distribution of \(r\), and sweep \(k\). If the differences between values of \(k\) disappear under radial control, the hypothesis is weakened.

6.3 What a random support does

A random support of size \(k\) changes how often a candidate touches the coordinates that matter, and how strongly it pushes them when it does. On average it does not change how much of the direction goes to those coordinates. Both halves of that statement use the same toy setting.

Take, for analysis only, an axis-aligned objective that depends on an active set \(A\) of coordinates with \(\lvert A \rvert = s\); the other \(d - s\) coordinates do not affect it. Draw the support \(S\) uniformly among the \(k\)-subsets of \(\{1, \ldots, d\}\). The overlap \(J = \lvert S \cap A \rvert\) counts how many active coordinates the support happens to include. Because the support is \(k\) coordinates drawn without replacement from \(d\), of which \(s\) are active, \(J\) is hypergeometric,

$$ P(J = j) = \frac{\binom{s}{j} \binom{d - s}{k - j}}{\binom{d}{k}}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[J] = \frac{k s}{d}, $$

and for \(s, k \ll d\) the chance of touching at least one active coordinate is about \(1 - e^{-k s / d}\) (Fact). Hypothetical instance with \(d = 500\), \(s = 10\), \(k = 20\): \(\mathbb{E}[J] = 0.4\), and the chance that a candidate touches any active coordinate is about 0.34 by the exact formula and 0.33 by the approximation. A small \(k\) means most candidates miss the active set entirely. This is the familiar argument against very sparse supports, and it is only half the story.

The other half is what a hit delivers. Write \(P_A\) for the projection onto the active coordinates, so \(\lVert P_A v \rVert^2\) is the fraction of the direction's squared length that lies in the active coordinates; the plan calls this quantity the squared active-space energy. Suppose the direction is isotropic on \(S\), meaning every direction within the support is equally likely. Then conditional on \(J = j\) the squared active-space energy has mean \(j / k\): the \(k\) coordinates of the support share the unit length equally on average, and \(j\) of them are active. Averaging over supports gives

$$ \mathbb{E}\lVert P_A v \rVert^2 = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \frac{J}{k} \Big] = \frac{s}{d} $$

for every \(k\) (Fact under isotropy). Hypothetical instance with \(d = 500\), \(s = 10\): the average squared active-space energy is 0.02 whether \(k\) is 20 or 500. Sparse and dense pools allocate the same average fraction of directional energy to an unknown active subspace, so “smaller \(k\) puts more energy into the active coordinates” is false on average.

What changes is the distribution of that energy across candidates. A dense direction gives every candidate a small active component, of size about \(1 / \sqrt{d}\). A sparse one gives most candidates none, and the rest a component of size about \(1 / \sqrt{k}\) (at \(d = 500\), \(k = 20\) a factor 5 larger). Sparse pools trade hit probability, which rises with \(k\), for concentration per hit, which rises as \(k\) falls.

Because the optimizer then takes the best candidate of a pool of \(M\), this is an extreme-value question: what matters is not the typical candidate but the best one in the pool, and the pool size \(M\) is part of the answer. Hypothesis: rare strong hits beat ubiquitous weak hits when the pool is large enough and the selector, the step that picks the best candidate from the pool, can find them. Step 0 (§4) makes this quantitative under the linear model. The tail diagnostic of Step 3 measures it: the distribution of the active-space displacement \(D_A = \lVert P_A (x - c) \rVert\) per \(k\), with its mass at zero and its upper quantiles.
