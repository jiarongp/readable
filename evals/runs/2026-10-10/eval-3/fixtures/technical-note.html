Constraint scoring note

2026-10-10 · 5 min read · status: draft · source: technical-note.md

This note explains how we score a candidate for feasibility: the probability that it satisfies every constraint at once. It covers the probability calculation behind the score, the batch implementation that scores 32 candidates in one call, and the limits of what the score means. The batch size is 32, and the reason for that choice is not recorded.

The evaluation cases accompany this note. See Feasibility for the probability calculation.

Feasibility

A candidate is feasible when it satisfies all of its constraints, and the feasibility score is the probability of that happening. This section introduces the objects in that sentence, then derives the score and the assumption it rests on.

Candidates and constraints

A candidate, written xx , is one proposed solution under consideration, such as one setting of the parameters we are free to choose. A constraint is a condition the solution must meet. There are mm constraints, and constraint jj is written as a function gjg_j of the candidate.

The convention is that constraint jj is satisfied when gj(x)≤0g_j(x) \leq 0 . Any condition can be put in this form by moving everything to one side. A hypothetical limit such as "mass at most 10 kg" becomes g(x)=mass(x)−10≤0g(x)=\mathrm{mass}(x)-10\leq 0 , and a candidate that is too heavy makes the left side positive. A candidate is feasible when it satisfies all mm constraints.

The score does not use the values of the constraint functions directly. Its input is one probability per constraint, P(gj(x)≤0∣x)P(g_j(x)\leq 0\mid x) : the chance that constraint jj is satisfied, given that the candidate is xx . The note calls these the satisfaction probabilities. It takes them as given and does not describe the models that produce them.

The product of satisfaction probabilities

Feasibility means that all mm constraint events happen together. The note calculates that probability under one assumption: conditional on the candidate xx , the constraint events are independent of each other. Under that assumption, the probability that they all happen is the product of their individual probabilities:

P(feasible∣x)=∏j=1mP(gj(x)≤0∣x)P(\mathrm{feasible}\mid x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}P(g_j(x)\leq 0\mid x)

Read the right-hand side as "multiply the mm satisfaction probabilities together." The left-hand side, P(feasible∣x)P(\mathrm{feasible}\mid x) , is the feasibility score of candidate xx .

A worked instance with hypothetical numbers: suppose a candidate has three constraints with satisfaction probabilities 0.9, 0.8, and 0.5. Its score is 0.9imes0.8imes0.5=0.360.9 imes 0.8 imes 0.5 = 0.36 .

Two properties follow from the multiplication. The score can never be higher than its smallest factor, so one doubtful constraint (the 0.5 here) caps the score on its own. And each extra constraint multiplies in another number that is at most 1, so adding a constraint can only keep the score the same or lower it.

The independence assumption

Two events are independent when knowing that one happened tells you nothing about whether the other happened. Here the independence is conditional on xx : once the candidate is fixed, learning that constraint 1 is satisfied must not change the probability that constraint 2 is satisfied. The rule P(AextandB)=P(A) P(B)P(A ext{ and } B)=P(A)\,P(B) holds only for independent events, so the product formula depends on this assumption.

Dependence between the constraint events can invalidate the calculation, and the error can go in either direction. A hypothetical example: suppose two constraints are really the same condition stated twice, each with probability 0.5. The true probability that both hold is 0.5, but the product gives 0.25, which is too low. Suppose instead that the two constraints can never hold at the same time. The true probability that both hold is 0, but the product still gives 0.25, which is too high.

The note states the assumption; it does not establish that the assumption holds for the constraints in use. The Limitations section returns to this point.

Batch operation

The implementation scores 32 candidates in one call rather than one at a time. Its input, output, and the one line of code between them are described below.

One row per candidate

The input probabilities is a two-dimensional array of shape (32, 3): 32 rows, one per candidate, and 3 columns, one per constraint. Row ii holds one candidate's three satisfaction probabilities P(gj(x)≤0∣x)P(g_j(x)\leq 0\mid x) , one column per constraint. The array counts rows from 0, while the formula counts constraints from 1.

def feasibility_score(probabilities):
    return probabilities.prod(axis=1)

The call prod(axis=1) multiplies along axis 1. In a two-dimensional array, axis 0 runs down the rows, from candidate to candidate, and axis 1 runs across the columns, from constraint to constraint. Multiplying along axis 1 collapses each row of 3 probabilities into a single number, so the result has shape (32,): one feasibility score per candidate, in the same order as the rows. Each score is exactly the product from the Feasibility section, computed for all 32 candidates in one operation.

The note does not name the array library. The shapes and the axis keyword follow NumPy's conventions, and the description above uses them.

What the code does not check

The function multiplies whatever probabilities it receives. Nothing in it tests whether the independence assumption is justified for these constraints, and the note does not establish that elsewhere. The batch scores therefore carry the same assumption as the single-candidate formula, and they inherit the same failure when the constraint events are dependent.

The batch size of 32 is fixed by the shape of the input. The reason for choosing 32 is not recorded.

Limitations

The score is a probability under the stated independence assumption. It is not a guarantee that the selected candidate will satisfy the constraints: a candidate chosen for its high score can still violate a constraint when it is evaluated. Two further limits follow from the sections above.

  • If the constraint events are dependent given the candidate, the product can be too high or too low, and neither the formula nor the implementation detects this.
  • The batch size is 32, and the reason for that choice is not recorded.
