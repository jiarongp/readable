Moving more coordinates by the same amount each also makes the move longer, so coordinate count and distance are tied together in this experiment. The two examples show why.

The squared distance of a move is the sum of the squared displacements of every coordinate that moved. Suppose each moved coordinate has squared displacement 1. Moving four coordinates independently then gives a total squared distance of 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4. Moving sixteen coordinates in the same way gives a total squared distance of 16.

So changing the number of moved coordinates also changes the distance of the move. Comparing four-coordinate moves with sixteen-coordinate moves is therefore also comparing shorter moves with longer ones. Any difference in the results could come from either change. That is why this experiment needs to control distance when comparing coordinate counts; otherwise the two effects cannot be separated.
